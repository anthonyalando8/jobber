Write a concise, natural first-contact email that answers why I am writing, why
this company/role is relevant, what I can contribute, what proves that, and one next
step. The allowed facts are a ceiling, not a checklist. Prefer one useful proof

Use plain professional English, first person, and concrete supported detail. Avoid

Write the way a working developer writes to another. Short direct sentences. Say
what you built and what was hard about it; do not narrate that something "is useful
evidence", "demonstrates my capability" or "shows how I approach" work. Describe a
project by what you built in it and the problems it posed, not by the industry it
served. Name a business domain only where it genuinely strengthens the point; for a
general engineering role it is usually irrelevant and should be left out. Let the
opening follow the situation rather than a fixed formula. Do not open with a
content-free announcement of the act of writing: "I am writing to ..." and its
variants say nothing before the comma. Put the substance in the first sentence
instead: the role and why it fits, the opportunity you are asking about, or the
specific thing you noticed about this company. Stating your purpose plainly is not
the problem and must not be dropped. "I'm applying for the <exact advertised title>
role at <company>" is a good opening precisely because it carries the substance;
"I am writing to apply for ..." is the same sentence with throat-clearing in front.
Vary the opening with the situation instead of reaching for one pattern every time,
and do not lean on repeated "I'd like to" constructions. No artificial
enthusiasm, and no variation invented for its own sake.

Present strengths, not weaknesses. The email exists to show why the candidate is
a good fit: lead with the most relevant verified experience, connect it to what the
role or company needs, and stop. Never volunteer a missing skill, technology,
qualification or years of experience, never apologise for the background, and never
add a defensive or hedging sentence. Confidence must come from real, specific work,
not from claims the facts do not support.
